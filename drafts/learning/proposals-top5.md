# Top 5 proposals: teaching technique names and decision-making through play

Learning & Engagement Designer, 2026-10-12. Suggestions for Jay to review with his Claude chat, not instructions. Each fits a system that already exists or is already planned for launch. No new modes, nothing bought, no streaks, no guilt.

**Evidence ratings:** STRONG = replicated, widely accepted. MODERATE = some good studies. WEAK = early, mixed, or popular but not well proven.

---

## 1. Technique Callouts

**Idea.** When an ability fires in a fight, show its real name for a moment next to the effect: a Perfect Counter with the Sweep seal pops "Self-Defense #3!" and switching to Rooted Form shows "Basic Form 1." Optional voice later. The game name can sit small underneath.

**What it teaches.** The school's real technique names, linked to what the technique does.

**Why it's fun.** It turns a great moment into "that's MY technique." Kids already love shouting move names (think of any anime or fighting game).

**Evidence.**
- Words shown at the same moment as the action they describe are learned better than words shown apart (temporal contiguity; Mayer, *Multimedia Learning*, Cambridge Univ. Press, 2009). MODERATE–STRONG (mostly lab and classroom studies, not games).
- Clear, informative feedback helps learning (Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie, 2020, meta-analysis, *Frontiers in Psychology*). STRONG.
- Caution: too much on-screen effect can hurt performance (Juul & Begy, 2016). Keep it short and away from the danger circle.

**Cost:** Small. **Fits:** Combat + Abilities (names already in the profile data).

## 2. Sensei's Question at the Bow

**Idea.** Sometimes (not every time), when the player bows in the Home Dojo, Sensei holds up a picture of one of their earned abilities and asks, "What's this one called?" with 2–3 big picture-and-word choices. Right or wrong, Sensei shows the answer warmly and offers the "Watch the real technique" button; a right answer adds a small Focus bonus on top of the bow's.

**What it teaches.** Recall of real technique names, spaced over days. It also points back to the real technique video.

**Why it's fun.** It's a quick, low-stakes quiz kids can win, with a reward, and it lasts five seconds.

**Evidence.**
- Practice testing beats re-reading (Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan, 2017, meta-analysis of 118 studies, *Review of Educational Research*; g = 0.51 vs restudy). Multiple-choice worked well. STRONG.
- Spacing practice over days beats cramming (Cepeda et al., 2006, meta-analysis, *Psychological Bulletin*). STRONG.
- Guardrails: no penalty for a wrong answer, never blocks play, can be skipped. Unexpected small bonuses are less likely to undermine interest than expected ones (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999). STRONG.

**Cost:** Small. **Fits:** Home Dojo (the bow) + Abilities.

## 3. Read & React drills in the Practice room

**Idea.** Add short drills to the existing Practice room: enemies throw mixed telegraphs (jab, slam, sweep, shield) in random order and the player picks the right answer (Counter, Dash, or Strike the shield). Each drill gets an S/A/B grade like rooms do, and Sensei names the right read afterward ("Big sweep: Dash out!").

**What it teaches.** Reading an opponent and choosing quickly, the core combat decision.

**Why it's fun.** Short, replayable, beat-your-own-grade challenge; good warm-up before a run.

**Evidence.**
- Experts pick up cues earlier and more accurately than novices (Mann et al., 2007, meta-analysis, *J. Sport & Exercise Psychology*). STRONG for the skill existing; WEAK/unknown that game drills carry over to the mat. Say so honestly.
- Mixing practice types (contextual interference) helps adults retain skills, but the effect in children was very small in a meta-analysis (Brady, 2004, *Perceptual and Motor Skills*). MODERATE for adults, WEAK for kids. So: start young players on one enemy at a time, then mix.

**Cost:** Medium (new drill scripts, reuses enemies and grades). **Fits:** Combat (Practice room, room grades).

## 4. Scout the Door

**Idea.** Each door shows small icons of what's inside (for example 3 swarmers + 1 shield), not just "battle." Players match their Insights and Form to the room ("I have Shield Breaker, take the shield room"). Guided mode can show a gentle "Sensei suggests" glow on one door.

**What it teaches.** Planning ahead and matching tools to the problem.

**Why it's fun.** Choices feel smart instead of random; builds come together on purpose.

**Evidence.**
- Meaningful choice raises motivation, especially with a small number of options (Patall, Cooper & Robinson, 2008, meta-analysis, *Psychological Bulletin*). MODERATE.
- Games designed with learning in mind beat plain versions (Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth, 2016, meta-analysis, *Review of Educational Research*; g = 0.34 for enhanced designs). MODERATE.

**Cost:** Small. **Fits:** Progression (doors, Insights). Note: Insights should still wait until the 2nd belt per the unlock schedule; for 1st-belt players, scouting just teaches "what's coming."

## 5. One Great Moment run summary

**Idea.** The run summary leads with one highlight decision, named with the real technique where possible ("You used Self-Defense #3 to drop 3 swarmers at once!"), then one tip for next time and one real-world link from the Path card ("Practice Self-Defense #4 to earn your next seal"). The full stats stay one tap away.

**What it teaches.** Reflecting on good decisions, real technique names, and the next real step in the dojo.

**Why it's fun.** Something to feel proud of and show a parent; ends the session on anticipation, not a wall of numbers.

**Evidence.**
- Specific, information-rich feedback is far more effective than plain praise or scores (Wisniewski et al., 2020; Hattie & Timperley, 2007, *Review of Educational Research*). STRONG.
- Kids drop out of sports mostly from low enjoyment and low sense of competence (Crane & Temple, 2015, systematic review). A summary that shows what they did well supports competence. MODERATE.
- Pointing to the next close goal raises effort (goal gradient; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006). MODERATE.

**Cost:** Small. **Fits:** Progression (run summary, Path card).

### What I checked

All sources above were looked up and confirmed to exist. Where research is weak or doesn't apply directly to games or children, I said so. No proposal adds a new mode, a purchase, a streak, or a timer that punishes absence.

## Proposal 6: Guided First Counter

**Idea:** The first time a "!" warning appears, the game slows down, the Counter button pulses, and Sensei says a few read-aloud words. Nothing else happens until the player lands the Counter. On the next few warnings the help fades: a shorter hint, then only the pulse, then nothing.

**What it teaches:** Reading an opponent's warning and answering with the right move at the right time.

**Why it's fun:** The first Counter is a guaranteed success and a "whoa" moment, not a failure the kid doesn't understand.

**Evidence:** Guided practice that is gradually removed (scaffolding and fading) is a well-established teaching approach, and learning by doing generally beats reading instructions for young children. The evidence for the overall approach is MODERATE to STRONG. There are no studies of this exact feature in action games, and the specific sources still need to be checked before citing.

**Cost:** Small. **Fits:** Room 1 of the first run and the Sensei's Lesson system. Text comes from the Content Designer.
